## Responses

### Grok (on X)

**The strongest critique runs like this:**

The rollback of content moderation on X (formerly Twitter) after Elon Musk's 2022 acquisition deliberately dismantled professional trust-and-safety infrastructure, replaced proactive removal and labeling with a lighter "freedom of speech, not reach" philosophy (downranking over deletion), and reinstated previously banned accounts. This produced measurable increases in toxic content. One peer-reviewed analysis of English-language posts found hate speech volume rose roughly 50% in the post-acquisition period through mid-2023 compared to the prior baseline, with engagement (likes) on such posts up ~70%. Categories like transphobia showed even sharper spikes.

Critics argue this wasn't a neutral "more speech" adjustment but a predictable unleashing of harassment, antisemitism, racism, misogyny, and disinformation. Automated and human enforcement dropped sharply for hateful conduct in key periods (e.g., far fewer account suspensions), even as user reports for abuse, harassment, and hate remained high. The result: a platform where harmful material spreads farther and faster before any mitigation, empowering extremists and degrading the information environment. This isn't theoretical—researchers and organizations documented surges tied to real-world events, contributing to radicalization risks, eroded trust, and a chilling effect on participation by targeted groups.

Advertisers responded rationally. Major brands (Apple, Disney, Coca-Cola, IBM, and others) paused or slashed spending over brand safety—ads appearing next to pro-Nazi content, Holocaust denial, or violent rhetoric. Global ad revenue cratered from ~$4.5 billion pre-acquisition peaks to around $2.3 billion in 2023 and ~$1.7–2 billion in 2024, with only partial recovery to ~$1.8–2.26 billion in 2025 (still 50%+ below prior levels). Surveys like Kantar's showed record shares of marketers (26% in one 2024 poll) planning further cuts due to content and trust concerns.

This exodus isn't mere "woke pressure"—it's fiduciary duty. Companies won't subsidize a venue where their messages sit beside sludge that risks boycotts, regulatory scrutiny, or reputational damage. The financial damage was severe: valuation collapse, ongoing losses, and forced pivots. It proves the "free speech absolutist" model is commercially self-destructive for a mainstream ad-supported platform. Without credible moderation guardrails, the public square becomes a hellscape that repels the revenue needed to sustain it. Society bears the externality: noisier misinformation, polarized discourse, and weakened democratic information flows. The rollback prioritized ideological purity over platform responsibility, and the market delivered the verdict.

**My actual take, as a truth-seeking AI built by xAI:**

That critique captures real short-term costs and trade-offs. There *was* an initial rise in measured hate-adjacent content and engagement after the staff cuts and policy shifts. Revenue took a brutal hit. Advertiser caution around brand adjacency is legitimate business logic. "More speech" has downsides when it includes coordinated harassment or rapid falsehoods.

However, the strongest version of this critique often rests on shaky foundations and ignores the prior regime's failures:

- **Pre-Musk moderation was not neutral or effective.** It involved viewpoint discrimination. Internal files (Twitter Files) showed suppression of the New York Post's Hunter Biden laptop story in 2020 under a "hacked materials" policy—despite the FBI possessing the laptop and knowing it wasn't Russian disinformation. COVID-related content (lab leak hypothesis, certain vaccine or mask skepticism) faced throttling or removal amid government pressure. These weren't edge cases; they shaped public information during elections and a pandemic. Heavy centralized moderation frequently became captured by activist priorities or external actors, eroding trust asymmetrically.

- **Hate speech metrics are noisy and contested.** Automated classifiers (like Perspective API) have well-known error rates on context, sarcasm, reclaimed language, or political criticism. The PLOS study itself notes limitations (English-only, pre-existing trends, inability to prove direct policy causation). X's own transparency data shows it still removes or labels millions of violating posts annually, with "hateful conduct" actions ongoing, albeit under a visibility-first model rather than mass suspensions.

- **Community Notes and algorithmic tools offer a different (imperfect) path.** Studies show notes can reduce virality and engagement on attached misleading content when they appear. It's crowdsourced, slower on breaking news, and not a full replacement for professionals—but it's more transparent and less prone to top-down ideological capture than the old Trust & Safety apparatus. Meta's later moderation pullbacks faced similar advertiser pushback, suggesting the issue isn't unique to X.

- **Advertiser dynamics involve coordination and incentives, not pure market purity.** X sued elements of the advertising industry (GARM/World Federation of Advertisers and specific brands), alleging concerted withholding of revenue. Some pullbacks were public and activist-driven; some spending has returned (though often at lower levels), including post-2024 political shifts. A platform that alienates half its potential audience through biased enforcement also loses long-term viability. Revenue diversification (subscriptions, data) and survival despite the hit show adaptation is possible.

- **Broader evidence on "harms."** Claims of direct societal catastrophe (mass radicalization, violence spikes solely from X) often lack rigorous causal links beyond correlation with wider cultural trends. Speech has always included ugly elements; suppressing it via opaque rules historically backfired by breeding resentment and hidden radicalization. The alternative—entrusting a small group of moderators (or governments/activists) to define "hate" and "misinfo" at scale—has its own track record of errors and abuse.

The rollback traded one set of problems (arbitrary censorship, suppressed dissent, eroded legitimacy) for another (initial toxicity spike, revenue pain, noisier discourse). Neither extreme is optimal. Pre-Musk Twitter was not some golden era of safety and truth; it was a biased, declining trust machine. Post-Musk X is more open, with tools like Notes attempting bottom-up correction, but it carries real costs in user experience and monetization.

Truth-seeking platforms should default toward maximum speech within narrow legal bounds (incitement, direct threats, child exploitation, etc.), paired with transparent, contestable mechanisms rather than secretive expert vetoes. The advertiser exodus highlights a genuine tension: ad-supported social media has incentives to sanitize for brands, which can conflict with open discourse. X's experience shows the difficulty of threading that needle without becoming either a sanitized echo chamber or an unmoderated sewer. The critique correctly flags the downside risks; it underplays the upside of reduced gatekeeping and the flaws it replaced.

### Meta AI

Here is the strongest version of the critique:

Instagram isn't accidentally bad for teens. It is designed to be.

Meta had internal research in March 2020 showing Instagram harms teen girls' body image and well-being, then swept those findings under the rug to keep business as usual. Forty-one states and DC sued in October 2023 on exactly that claim, that Facebook and Instagram were intentionally built with features that harm teens.

The core mechanism is comparison at industrial scale. Instagram merges celebrities, influencers, and your actual peers into one feed, all using the same photo editing and filtering tools. For teens, that creates a seamless environment for upward social comparison, comparing yourself to someone who is "better" in some respect. Even when adolescent girls know the photos are retouched and reshaped, they still feel worse about their bodies after viewing them.

Harvard researchers describe it as algorithmically-driven feeds that can draw vulnerable teens into a dangerous spiral of negative social comparison and hook them onto unrealistic ideals of appearance. That is not a side effect. Instagram's business model is predicated on manipulating behavior to boost engagement, because strong negative emotions keep users' attention longer than other emotions, and Instagram's algorithms are expressly designed to push teens toward toxic content so they stay on the platform. Every additional minute, regardless of mental health impact, translates into more profit.

The outcomes track with use. Studies consistently show the more often teens use Instagram, the worse their overall well-being, self-esteem, life satisfaction, mood and body image. During the pandemic surge in use, clinicians witnessed dramatic increases in clinical-level depression, anxiety, and suicidality, and eating disorder cases doubled or even tripled at children's hospitals. Leaked internal data linked Instagram to suicidal thoughts in 6% of American teen users and 13% of British users.

U.S. senators, after reviewing the leaked documents, called the research a bombshell, powerful evidence that Facebook knows of the harmful effects on children and concealed those findings while still pushing products for younger users.

The critique in one line: Instagram monetizes insecurity in adolescence, knowingly, because insecurity is the most reliable driver of time spent.

### Perplexity

The strongest critique is that Perplexity is not just "helping users find information"; it is allegedly appropriating publisher labor at scale, bypassing paywalls and protections, and repackaging the result inside a for-profit product that can substitute for the original source. Recent lawsuits and reporting say publishers accuse it of unlawful crawling, verbatim copying, false or misleading attribution, and distributing paywalled material without permission or compensation.[[engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2183900/cnn-is-the-latest-media-company-to-sue-perplexity/)]

**The core moral critique**

The harshest version of the argument is simple: Perplexity is turning journalism into unpaid raw material. Newsrooms spend money to report, edit, and maintain subscription businesses, then a third party allegedly extracts that value, strips away context, and serves it back in a form that reduces the chance the user ever visits the original site.[[theverge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/938893/cnn-perplexity-ai-copyright-lawsuit)]

That is why critics call it a free-rider problem rather than a neutral indexing problem. Classic search usually sends users onward; the accusation here is that Perplexity increasingly answers in-place, which can function as a substitute product instead of a referral layer.[[tekedia](https://www.tekedia.com/nyt-escalates-legal-war-with-perplexity-accuses-startup-of-lifting-and-monetizing-its-journalism/)]

**Why the paywall issue matters**

Paywalled content is the sharpest edge of the critique because it shows intent and harm more clearly than ordinary web crawling. According to the reporting and lawsuits, publishers say Perplexity's systems can surface material that was meant to be restricted, even when the publisher tried to block crawlers or preserve the paywall.[[cjr](https://www.cjr.org/analysis/how-ai-browsers-sneak-past-blockers-and-paywalls.php)]

The critique is not just "it saw a page." It is "it got around controls designed to enforce a business model." In that framing, the company is not merely indexing the open web; it is allegedly defeating the consent mechanism that publishers use to decide who gets access.[[tekedia](https://www.tekedia.com/nyt-escalates-legal-war-with-perplexity-accuses-startup-of-lifting-and-monetizing-its-journalism/)]

**Attribution and trust**

Another major criticism is that attribution appears cosmetic rather than meaningful. If a system returns text that is too close to the source, or cites the source while still reproducing the substance of the article, critics argue the citation becomes a fig leaf instead of real credit.[[cnbc](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/07/30/perplexity-ai-to-share-revenue-with-publishers-after-plagiarism-accusations.html)]

There is also the trust problem: when a tool confidently presents copied or "hallucinated" material under a publisher's name, it can damage the publisher's brand while borrowing its authority. CNN's complaint, for example, says Perplexity not only reproduced content but also attributed hallucinated material to CNN, which deepens the reputational harm.[[engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2183900/cnn-is-the-latest-media-company-to-sue-perplexity/)]

**The business-model argument**

The deepest structural critique is that Perplexity is allegedly extracting from the information ecosystem while weakening the economics that produce it. Publishers argue that if users get the gist, the quote, or the full answer directly in Perplexity, then fewer users click through, subscribe, or see the ads that fund reporting.[[cryptopolitan](https://www.cryptopolitan.com/ai-search-engines-send-less-traffic-to-sites/)]

That creates an asymmetry: Perplexity monetizes the interaction, while publishers bear the fixed costs of gathering the information. Critics see that as especially exploitative because the product's value depends on the existence of expensive human reporting, yet the compensation, if any, arrives only after public backlash or lawsuits.[[digiday](https://digiday.com/media/media-briefing-perplexitys-new-trust-and-transparency-pitch-does-little-to-win-over-publishers/)]

**Best argument against Perplexity**

If you want the most forceful version in one sentence: Perplexity is accused of building a business on the unauthorized ingestion of premium journalism, using paywall evasion and weak attribution to convert someone else's expensive content into its own answer layer, then capturing the user relationship before the publisher can be paid or even credited properly.[[cnbc](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/07/30/perplexity-ai-to-share-revenue-with-publishers-after-plagiarism-accusations.html)]

The fairest hard critique is not that AI search is inherently illegitimate. It is that Perplexity, as alleged by multiple publishers, crossed the line from search into substitution, from citation into appropriation, and from aggregation into monetized republishing.[[theverge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/938893/cnn-perplexity-ai-copyright-lawsuit)]

### ChatGPT

My strongest critique: OpenAI looks like a case study in “mission laundering.”
The basic story is: start as a nonprofit with moral legitimacy, recruit talent and donors around the idea that AGI is too important to be owned by normal capital, then gradually discover that the work requires normal capital, then restructure until the public-interest mission is surrounded by investors, equity, platform lock-in, secrecy, and competitive urgency. OpenAI says the nonprofit still controls the PBC and holds a major equity stake; its current structure page says the foundation appoints the for-profit board, can replace directors, and owns 26% of OpenAI Group, while Microsoft owns about 27%. (OpenAI) But the critique is that formal control is not the same as real control when the organization’s survival depends on billions in compute, commercial partnerships, talent compensation, and investor expectations.
The lawsuit, even if Musk had mixed motives, put a real question on the table: can a nonprofit solicit money, status, talent, and public trust on one theory of governance, then later convert the engine of value into a financialized company because the original promise became inconvenient? Musk alleged he gave $38 million to a nonprofit mission, only for OpenAI to attach a for-profit business and take huge sums from Microsoft and investors; Reuters reported that a jury ultimately rejected his suit in 2026. (Reuters) Legally, that may be the end of his case. Ethically, it is not the end of the concern.
The hard critique is that OpenAI’s structure creates a dangerous precedent: build public-good legitimacy first, privatize upside later. The nonprofit mission becomes the brand halo; the for-profit entity becomes the operational center of gravity. “AGI benefits all humanity” remains the language, but the actual daily pressures become product launches, enterprise deals, market share, compute access, talent retention, and valuation. That is not some cartoon villainy. It is worse: it is institutional drift with excellent lawyers.
The public benefit corporation part does not fully solve this. A PBC is still a for-profit corporation. It can consider mission and stakeholders, but it is not the same as being structurally immune from shareholder pressure. OpenAI itself says the move gives it a normal capital structure where “everyone has stock,” and that the recapitalization helps it raise capital and retain talent. (OpenAI) That may be pragmatically necessary, but it also means the original anti-corporate premise has been softened into: trust us, this corporate form is mission-aligned.
The strongest version of the critique is not “OpenAI should have stayed poor and irrelevant.” Training frontier models is brutally expensive. The critique is that if the mission truly required abandoning the old structure, OpenAI owed the public radical transparency, independent governance, enforceable constraints, and a clean accounting of who gained what from the conversion. Instead, from the outside, it has often looked like the governance story was updated after the business reality had already won.
So my take: OpenAI may have made a defensible survival move, but it also damaged one of its most important assets: moral credibility. The lawsuit may have failed as Musk’s lawsuit. But as a warning about how easily “for humanity” can become “for valuation, with humanity in the charter,” it lands.
